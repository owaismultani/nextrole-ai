# Messages: templates, limits and lint

Every message the skill drafts, with a before/after for each. Placeholders in `<>` are filled only from the resume, the posting, saved career facts or the user's answers. If no fact backs a sentence, drop the sentence. Every draft is labelled a draft; the user sends it.

## 1. Principles

| Rule                                                                                       | Why                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| One ask, as a yes/no question                                                              | Emails with one to three questions got more replies than those with none (vendor data, correlational); one clear ask is easiest to say yes to.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| Connection first                                                                           | The first clause says why this person: an alum, a mutual contact, their talk or post, a referral from someone.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| Short                                                                                      | Cold email 50–125 words (aim under 100); LinkedIn note ≤200 characters; thank-you 75–150 words.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| More than half the words about them                                                        | Steve Dalton's "5-Point Email" (The 2-Hour Job Search).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Ask strangers for information, not a job                                                   | Ask for a referral only after a conversation: "directions, not a ride" (UC Berkeley Career Engagement).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Plain words, short concrete subject lines (3–4 words did best in one 2016 vendor analysis) | Plain, short email did best in the same analysis; treat it as a heuristic, not a reading-level target.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| Aim at moderately weak ties                                                                | In a LinkedIn experiment with about 20M users, people with some mutual connections helped job moves most, especially in digital industries.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Ask for referrals where a programme exists                                                 | Across nine firms, referred applicants were hired and accepted offers at higher rates (Burks et al. 2015). Referrals are not expected everywhere.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| Thank helpers                                                                              | Thanks makes helpers more willing to help again (Grant & Gino 2010), and recipients value it more than senders expect (Kumar & Epley 2018).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Specific beats polished                                                                    | After LLMs arrived, employers on one freelance platform stopped paying a premium for tailored-looking applications (Galdin & Silbert 2025), and people suspected of AI-written replies were judged more harshly (Hohenstein et al. 2023). Writing assistance on resumes raised hires by about 8% in one large field experiment (Wiles et al.), so help with clarity is fine; every message still needs a detail only this user could write. |
| Never invent                                                                               | No metric, mutual contact, shared experience or "I loved your post" that the user hasn't given you.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |

## 2. Channel limits

LinkedIn changes these without notice; treat them as "as of 2026" and check in the product when it matters.

| Channel                                | Limit                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| LinkedIn connection note, free account | 200 characters (UC Berkeley's current guidance). The free monthly allowance of personalised notes is small and unpublished; if the user hits it, send the invite without a note and message after they accept.                                         |
| LinkedIn connection note, Premium      | 300 characters (widely reported, unverified)                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| Messaging a non-connection             | Only by InMail (Premium) or to members with Open Profile; otherwise wait for the connection to be accepted before following up                                                                                                                         |
| InMail                                 | Premium only. Premium Career gets 5 credits a month, rolling over to a maximum of 15, refunded if the recipient replies within 90 days; you can't InMail the same person again until they reply (LinkedIn Help). Keep the body under 100 words anyway. |
| Email                                  | No limit. Use a subject line and plain text. No attachments on a cold first touch, except a resume when asked for or when requesting a referral.                                                                                                       |

Count characters for every LinkedIn note and print the count. Over 200? Cut the context clause before the ask.

## 3. Drafting and lint

**Drafting steps.**

1. Trigger: what happened (applied, interviewed, offer, silence).
2. Recipient: name, role, relationship (strong, moderate, weak, none).
3. Channel and its limit.
4. Facts: posting, resume sent, interview notes and participants, saved career facts. Ask the user for one personal specific when there is none.
5. Draft to the template; lint; show the word or character count.
6. At most two variants.
7. After "sent": log `Sent: …`, set the follow-up, apply any stage change (pipeline-rules.md §4).

**Lint: rewrite on any hit.**

1. Stock openers and closers: "I hope this message finds you well", "I came across your profile", "I'd love to pick your brain", "Looking forward to hearing from you!", "Thank you for your time and consideration" (on cold notes).
2. Vague praise: "impressive journey", "inspiring work", "truly passionate", with no object the user has actually read or heard. Ask; never invent one.
3. Buzzwords: leverage, synergy, results-driven, dynamic, spearheaded, delve, tapestry; "thrilled" or "excited" opening every message.
4. Rhythm tells: three-item lists everywhere, symmetric paragraphs, chains of dashes, every sentence the same length.
5. Over-length or more than one ask.
6. Wrong register: cover-letter formality in a LinkedIn message; no contractions in a casual English channel.
7. Batch sameness: two drafts to different people sharing most sentences.
8. Fabrication risk: any metric, mutual contact or shared experience not in the user's material. Block and ask.

Fix pattern: keep the user's own phrases, use first-person contractions, include one detail only they could know, read it aloud, cut 20%.

## 4. Networking and referrals

### Cold networking email

Shape: connection (one sentence) → why them, with a specific the user has actually read → who the user is, one fact → one yes/no ask with a time bound → easy out.

> **Before:** "I hope this message finds you well! I came across your impressive profile and was truly inspired by your journey. As a passionate, results-driven engineer, I'd love to pick your brain and explore potential synergies…"
>
> **After** (subject: `Waterloo CS alum: payments infra question`): "Hi Priya, I'm a fellow Waterloo CS grad. Your write-up on cutting reconciliation time at Acme is the clearest explanation of ledger design I've read. I've spent four years on card-retry systems at Beta and I'm weighing a move toward payments infrastructure. Would you be open to a 20-minute call in the next few weeks? Totally understand if now's busy. Sam"

The "before" has no verifiable specific, three clichés, no clear ask and is all about the sender.

### LinkedIn connection note

> **Before** (234 characters, generic): "Hi Priya, I hope you're well! I came across your profile and I'm really impressed by your experience in payments. I'm passionate about fintech and would love to connect and learn more about your journey. Looking forward to connecting!"
>
> **After** (187 characters): "Hi Priya, fellow Waterloo CS alum. Your post on cutting Acme ledger reconciliation time stuck with me. I'm exploring payments-infra roles. Open to a 20-min chat in the next few weeks? Sam"

Another (187 characters): "Hi Priya, we both spoke at PyCon AU. I'm moving from analytics to data engineering, and your analyst-to-DE-lead path is the one I'm weighing. Could I ask 3 questions on a 20-min call? Sam"

### Informational interview

Ask for 20–30 minutes and propose a window, not a slot. Don't attach a resume or name a job ID in the first message. Prepare 3–5 open questions; keep to the agreed time; near the end ask "Who else should I talk to?" and whether you may stay in touch. Thank them within 1–2 days, and send a short update later if their advice led somewhere.

### Referral ask, with a forwardable blurb

Only after some rapport, and where the company has a referral programme. Make it zero effort for the referrer: role title, req ID, link, resume, and a third-person blurb of 30–50 words with one fact the user has confirmed.

> **Before:** "Hey! Could you refer me to your company? I'd really appreciate it. My resume is attached."
>
> **After:** "Thanks again for the time last week; your point about on-call ownership changed how I read the role. I'm applying for Senior Backend Engineer, Payments (req 4821). If you're comfortable, would you refer me? A blurb you can paste is below, plus my resume. If it's not possible, no problem at all.
>
> _Blurb:_ Sam Lee, backend engineer, 6 years in payments. At Beta he rebuilt card-retry logic (failed charges down 31%) and led a 4-person on-call rotation. Applying for Senior Backend Engineer, Payments (req 4821): <link>. Resume attached."

If they say no, thank them; a no often reflects policy. If yes, ask: "Should I apply first, or wait for your submission?" Never name someone as a referrer before they agree.

## 5. Recruiters and applications

### Replying to a recruiter

| Situation      | Pattern                                                                                                                        |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Interested     | Thanks → one line of fit (a real fact) → 2–3 time windows with timezone → ask for the pay range if the posting doesn't show it |
| Not now        | Thanks → "not the right time because <brief true reason>" → "I'd welcome a note in <month>, especially for <type of role>"     |
| Not interested | Two sentences. Optionally suggest a peer, with that peer's consent                                                             |

### Post-application follow-up (named contact only)

At most 80 words, 7–10 business days after applying, adding one fact the resume doesn't make obvious.

> **Before:** "Just checking in on my application, any updates?"
>
> **After** (subject `Senior Backend Engineer (req 4821): Sam Lee`): "Hi Dana, I applied for Senior Backend Engineer on 2 Oct. The role's focus on retries and idempotency is what I built at Beta, where failed charges fell 31%. Is the role still open? Thanks, Sam"

No content, no message: "just checking in" alone doesn't earn a reply. Without a named person, don't chase the ATS; look for a contact instead.

## 6. Interviews: thank-you, status check, expedite

### Thank-you notes

- **When:** within 24 hours; same day is best. Email, not handwritten: decisions often come within days.
- **Who:** one note per interviewer when you have their address; otherwise one note to the recruiter that names each person. Notes get compared, so no two may be alike.
- **Content:** thanks → one callback to something that interviewer actually said (ask the user; never invent one) → optionally one addition or correction → one line of interest. 75–150 words.
- **Expectations:** no controlled studies show thank-you notes change outcomes; the popular survey numbers are vendor polls. They're cheap, courteous and mildly positive. Never suggest one fixes a weak interview.

**Worked example (no MCP):** "Write thank-you notes to the three people I interviewed with yesterday."

Ask once, as a batch: (0) the company, the role and how you sign off, if I don't have them; (1) their names and roles, and email addresses or the recruiter's; (2) for each person, one thing they said or asked that stuck with you; (3) anything you'd add or correct; (4) when they said you'd hear back.

Suppose the user answers: Senior Backend Engineer at Acme, signs "Sam". Dana Ruiz, hiring manager, explained the team owns its own on-call (user: "same as at Beta, and it's my favourite part"). Wei Chen, staff engineer, asked about a database migration the user undersold (actual: old and new schemas ran side by side for two weeks, cutover downtime cut from 4 hours to 20 minutes, a rollback plan existed) and described the team's idempotency-key design. Omar Haddad, product manager, asked how to weigh reliability work against features (user: "at Beta we tied every reliability ticket to a customer-facing incident"). Decision "by Tuesday 13 Oct".

> **To Dana Ruiz** (subject `Thank you: Senior Backend Engineer`, 76 words): "Hi Dana, thank you for the time yesterday. Hearing that your team owns its own on-call made the role much clearer to me. That's how we worked at Beta, and it's the part of the job I enjoy most. I came away more interested in the role than before. I understand a decision is expected by Tuesday; I look forward to hearing from you, and please let me know if anything else would help. Best, Sam"
>
> **To Wei Chen** (subject `Migration question follow-up`, 77 words): "Hi Wei, thanks for the technical conversation yesterday. I undersold my answer on the database migration, so here is the missing piece: we ran the old and new schemas side by side for two weeks and cut the final cutover downtime from four hours to twenty minutes. I'd be glad to walk through the rollback plan if that's useful. I also enjoyed hearing how your team designs its idempotency keys. Thanks again for your time. Best, Sam"
>
> **To Omar Haddad** (subject `Reliability vs features`, 58 words): "Hi Omar, thank you for making time yesterday. Your question about weighing reliability work against features stayed with me. At Beta we tied every reliability ticket to a customer-facing incident, and I'd like to hear how your team makes that call. I'd enjoy working through it with you. Thanks again, and I hope we speak soon. Best, Sam"

Check before showing: every clause about the user's past, the employer or what an interviewer said maps to one of these answers or to the resume. Thanks and a line of interest are the only additions. Delete any other clause, even if a note drops below 75 words. Past 24 hours, send today anyway, with no apology for the delay. After the user says they're sent: log `Sent: thank-you to Dana Ruiz (hiring manager), email` (one note per person) and set the follow-up to Thu 15 Oct (their date plus two business days).

### Status check after a missed date

> **Before:** "Hi, it's been a while. Have you made a decision yet?"
>
> **After:** "Hi Dana, you mentioned the team hoped to decide by Tue 13 Oct. Is there an updated timeline for the Senior Backend Engineer role? I'm still very interested, and I can share a code sample or a reference sooner if that helps. Thanks, Sam"

At most two status checks: the second 5 business days after the first. If the second gets no reply within 10 business days, propose `closed` / `no-response`.

### Expedite (another offer has a deadline)

> "Hi Dana, a quick update: I've received an offer with a decision deadline of Fri 16 Oct. I'm very interested in <role> at <Company>. Is there any chance of an update before then? Happy to make myself available for any remaining steps."

Say "first choice" to at most one company, and only if it is true. Every other expedite note says "strong interest". Send expedite notes only to processes the user would pick over the offer in hand. Never invent a competing offer or a deadline.

## 7. Endings: rejection, withdrawal

### Reply to a rejection

Thanks (one sentence) → optionally one narrow feedback question ("Was there a skill area that weighed most?", never "Why didn't I get it?") → door open, naming a type of role. Many employers decline to give feedback; a non-answer is normal. Never argue or ask them to reconsider.

> **Before:** "I'm really disappointed. Can you tell me why I was rejected? I think I was a great fit."
>
> **After:** "Thank you for letting me know, and for the time the team spent with me. If you can share it, was there one area that weighed most in the decision? I'd welcome hearing about future backend roles on the payments team. Best, Sam"

### Withdrawal

Send as soon as the user decides, before the next scheduled step. Brief; a reason only if true and kind; thanks to the people who invested time.

> **Before:** "Sorry, I'm no longer interested."
>
> **After:** "Hi Dana, I'm writing to withdraw from consideration for the Senior Backend Engineer role. I've accepted another position. Thank you, and Wei and Omar, for the time and the thoughtful conversations; I'd be glad to stay in touch. Best, Sam"

No details are owed. Then close the record as `withdrew` or `accepted-other`.

## 8. Offers: accept, decline, extension

Fallback templates. offer-negotiation, if installed, drafts these with its full term checklist (bonus, equity, sign-on clawback, notice, covenants) and owns the counter; this skill logs what was sent and runs the accept cascade.

### Accept

Reply in writing restating title, base pay, start date, location and any negotiated terms; ask for an updated written offer if terms changed; ask about next steps (paperwork, background check). Then draft withdrawals for every other active process.

> "Hi Dana, I'm delighted to accept the offer for Senior Backend Engineer: base €95,000, start date 2 Nov, remote from Lisbon, as set out in your letter of 9 Oct. Could you send the updated contract, and let me know the next steps for paperwork and the background check? Thank you, Sam"

Every figure here must come from the written offer.

### Decline

Promptly. Thanks → the decline in the first two lines → an optional one-line reason, with no comparison that insults them → door open.

> **Before:** "After careful consideration of all factors, I have decided that this opportunity isn't the right fit at this time, as another company offered a better package."
>
> **After:** "Hi Dana, thank you for the offer and for how smoothly the process ran. After a lot of thought, I've decided to decline; I've accepted a role closer to my current focus. I enjoyed meeting the team and hope our paths cross again. Best, Sam"

### Extension request

Ask before the deadline. Give a specific date and a true reason, and restate interest. Don't say you want time to land a better offer: it signals low interest (UC Berkeley).

> **Before:** "Can I have more time? I'm waiting to hear from other companies."
>
> **After:** "Hi Dana, thank you again; I'm excited about this offer. To make a decision I can fully commit to, could I have until Fri 23 Oct? I have a final-round interview on 20 Oct that I committed to before your offer, and I want to close that out properly. I'll tell you as soon as I've decided."

## 9. Salary questions

**"What are your salary expectations?"** Default: ask for the range first ("Could you share the budgeted range for the role?"). If the posting shows a range, anchor in its upper part with a scope reason: "Given <scope>, I'd expect to be in the upper part of the posted range, depending on the full package." If pressed with no range and the user has solid data, give a researched range that starts at their target, never their walk-away figure (with weak data, ask for the band or defer until the scope is clear): "Based on market data for <role> in <city>, I'm targeting <X–Y> base, depending on scope and total compensation." The numbers come from the user's research; never supply market figures you can't source.

The "never name a number first" rule is contested: first offers anchor negotiations in the first mover's favour (Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001), so a well-researched, ambitious range can beat repeated deflection. Expect the question in the recruiter screen; it has moved earlier in hiring.

**"What is your current salary?"** As of 2026, 22 US states and about 24 localities bar or limit the question, and the EU Pay Transparency Directive bans it once transposed nationally (the deadline was 7 June 2026; implementation is uneven). The user decides whether to share; most bans let candidates volunteer. Script anywhere: "I'd rather focus on what this role is worth; my expectation is <range>." Don't lecture recruiters about the law. Where the question is routine and lawful (e.g. "current CTC, expected CTC, notice period" on Indian forms), answer plainly if the user chooses to.

Choosing the number in depth (weak data, India CTC, 12 or 13 payments, public pay scales) and any negotiation belong to offer-negotiation if installed; otherwise use SKILL.md §6 "Light negotiation". Never state a current or competing pay figure the user doesn't actually have.

## 10. Regional formality

Convention, not research; ask the user about local norms when unsure.

- **Germany:** first contact uses _Sie_ and "Sehr geehrte Frau / Sehr geehrter Herr <surname>", with academic titles (Dr.), until invited otherwise.
- **Japan:** formal and indirect; hiring often runs through agents, so a direct cold ask can misfire.
- **India:** corporate email often opens "Dear Sir/Ma'am"; CTC and notice-period questions are routine.
- **Netherlands, Israel, Australia:** more directness is fine.
- **US tech:** first names and brevity.
- **Thank-you notes:** a strong US norm; in the UK, much of continental Europe and Japan a brief email is fine but less expected, and effusive notes can read oddly.

Rule: mirror the recipient's last message (greeting, sign-off, length). When in doubt, go one notch more formal on first contact.

## 11. Myths

- "80% of HR managers find thank-you notes helpful but only 24% receive them" and "68% of hiring managers say thank-you notes matter": vendor polls with thin methods; don't quote them.
- "Handwritten notes stand out more": no evidence, and they arrive after decisions.
- "Follow up every few days to show persistence": no evidence; career centres say one follow-up after about a week.
- "Weak ties get you jobs": oversimplified; moderately weak ties help most, and less so in non-digital industries.
- "LinkedIn notes allow 300 characters": outdated for free accounts.
- "AI detectors can tell": unreliable. The real risk is people judging text they suspect is AI-written.
- "Salary-history questions are illegal in the US": a state and city patchwork.
