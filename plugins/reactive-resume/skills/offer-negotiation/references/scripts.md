## 1. Early salary-expectation questions

Pick the move from SKILL.md's table first; these are the words.

- **Range posted:** "I've seen the range of <A–B>. Given <scope or level evidence>, I'd expect to be in the upper part of it, around <target>, depending on the full package. Could you share how bonus and equity are structured?"
- **No range:** "Could you share the budgeted range for this role? Then I can tell you quickly whether we're aligned."
- **Pressed, with solid data:** "The data I've seen (<source, date>) puts <level> in <location> at <data range>. Given <scope>, I'm targeting <target–target+>, and I'm flexible on how the package is structured." The bottom of the user's range is the target, never the walk-away point, and the data range is quoted as the source gives it.
- **Pressed, weak data:** "I'd like to understand the scope and the full package before I put a number on it. What range has the team budgeted?"
- **Pay history where it's banned:** "I'd rather focus on what this role is worth. My expectation for it is <target>."
- **Pay history where it's lawful:** declining is still allowed with the same line. If the user chooses to share, state the true figure plainly.
- **India ("current CTC, expected CTC, notice"):** "My current CTC is <true total>: <fixed> fixed, <variable> variable, <other components>. For this role I'm looking at <expected>, based on <market source or scope>. My notice period is <N> days; <buyout terms, if any>."
- **Germany:** "Meine Gehaltsvorstellung liegt bei <figure> Euro brutto im Jahr, <bei 12 / bei 13> Gehältern." In English: "<figure> euros gross a year, assuming <12 or 13> payments."
- **EU, range not given:** "Could you share the pay range for the role? The EU pay transparency rules ask employers to share it before interviews." Only where national law has transposed it, say it's a legal right (countries.md §4).

Before: "I'm on 65k now, so maybe 70k?" After: "Could you share the band for this role? From <source> for <level> in <city>, I'm targeting <figure> euros gross a year, assuming <12 or 13> payments, depending on the package."

## 2. The verbal offer call and the recap

On the call (don't accept, counter or ask "when do I start?"):

> "Thank you, that's great news. I'm excited about <team or problem>. Could you walk me through base, bonus, equity, sign-on and the start date? And could you send it in writing, with the date you need an answer by?"

"That's interesting; I'd like to look at the whole package" is a safe non-committal reaction. Same day, a recap email:

> "Hi <name>, thanks for the call today. To make sure I have it right: <each term as said>. I'll come back to you by <date>. Could you send the written offer when it's ready? Best, <user>"

## 3. Asking for missing terms

Send the questions before the counter, in one email, so the counter rests on facts:

> "Hi <name>, thank you again for the offer. Before I respond, could you help with a few details? 1. <What did the bonus pay out as a % of target in the last two years?> 2. <What's the vesting schedule, and is there a refresher policy?> 3. <Is the remote arrangement stated in the contract?> Thanks, <user>"

Private equity questions: decoding.md §3. India: ask for the payslip-level breakup under the current Labour Code structure.

## 4. Extension requests and exploding offers

Ask before the deadline, once, with a specific date and a true reason. Never say "to see what else comes in".

> "Hi <name>, thank you; I'm seriously considering this and want to give you a considered yes. I have <true reason: a final round on 20 Oct I'd committed to / a notice discussion with my current employer> finishing on <date>. Could we set the deadline to <date>? I'll update you as soon as I've decided."

- Exploding offer (a deadline of days, or a bonus that shrinks the longer you wait): same script, and suggest a middle date. Many employers grant extensions; some treat short deadlines as a tactic. NACE calls 1–2 weeks common for US graduate offers and says shorter can be undue pressure; that is a US campus norm.
- Refused: decide on the fallback as it stands today, never on offers the user hopes for. Never let a deadline pass in silence.
- To speed up other processes the user would pick over this offer (only those): "I've received an offer with a decision deadline of <date>. I'm very interested in <role> at <company>; is there any way to speed up your process?" "First choice" goes to at most one company, and only if true. job-application-manager logs these.

## 5. The counter email

Full example (the user's figures: data from <source, date>, a documented forfeited vest):

> Subject: Senior Engineer offer: a few points before I sign
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> Thank you again. I'm excited about the payments reliability work and want to make this work.
>
> Before I accept, I'd like to ask about two things:
>
> 1. Base: 200,000 USD. The verified data I've found for this level in Seattle (Levels.fyi, October 2026) puts base mostly between 190,000 and 205,000.
> 2. A sign-on of 30,000 USD. Joining means forfeiting 30,000 USD of RSUs that vest at my current employer in February.
>
> If base has less room, I'd be equally happy with base 192,000 USD plus that 30,000 USD sign-on.
>
> If we can get there, I'm ready to sign this week.
>
> Best, Sam

**No competing offer.** Don't mention other processes at all, or say so plainly when asked:

> "I don't have another offer in hand; this is the role I want. Given <posted range / market data> and <scope>, <figure> would let me commit fully."

**With a real competing offer.** Accurate and verifiable; name the company only if the user wants to (naming adds credibility but invites verification):

> "I have a written offer from <company or 'a public company'> at <base / bonus / equity as written>, expiring <date>. I'd prefer <your company> because <true reason>. Can you get closer to <target>?"

A verbal competing offer is described as verbal. Share the letter only if the user wants to: check its confidentiality wording first and never edit it; a redacted excerpt or the recruiter's email is often enough.

**Relational line.** For any user, one sentence that ties the ask to the relationship costs little and helps: "I want to come in fully committed, and getting this right lets me do that." Don't add a second.

## 6. The counter call

1. Thanks and enthusiasm: "Thanks for the offer; I'm excited about <specific>."
2. Frame: "I have a few questions to make sure the full package works."
3. Each ask with its reason, all at once. Then **stop talking**; let the silence sit.
4. On a no: "Understood. What flexibility is there on <next item>?"
5. Close: "Could you send the updated terms in writing?" Recap by email the same day (§2).

If the user prefers email only, send §5 and offer a call: "Happy to talk it through if that's easier."

## 7. Their pushback

- **They come back partway.** "Thank you, that's a real move. If <one item> can reach <figure>, I'm ready to sign as soon as it's in writing." Only if it's true; one closing ask, never reopening agreed items.
- **"This is our best and final offer."** "Understood, and thanks for checking. Is there flexibility on <one non-base item: sign-on / a level review at 6 months in writing / start date / equity>? Either way I'll confirm by <date>." Then decide; don't counter base again.
- **"That's above our band" / "the budget is fixed."** "That makes sense. Within the band, could we look at <sign-on / level / start date>?" Move to what the hiring manager controls.
- **"Everyone at this level is paid the same."** "Thanks, that's helpful. Then is the level right for the scope we discussed, <scope evidence>? And is there room on <sign-on or start date>?"
- **"Can you send me the other offer?"** "Yes, I can share <what's true and what the user is comfortable sharing>." Or: "I'd rather keep the letter confidential, but the terms are <accurate summary>."
- **"If I get you to X, will you sign today?"** Yes only if X clears the target and the user truly will: "If you can do X, I'll sign as soon as I have it in writing." Otherwise: "I'd want to see the full terms in writing first, and I'll respond by <date>."
- **"Are we your top choice?"** Answer the intent honestly: "This is the role I most want, and I'd like to make the numbers work" only if true; otherwise "I'm very interested, and I'm deciding by <date>."
- **Silence past their date.** "Hi <name>, just checking on timing for the updated terms. Is there anything you need from me?"
- **An ultimatum.** Never issue one. When receiving one, stay calm and keep to facts and dates.

## 8. Public-sector step request

US federal (GS), before accepting the tentative offer:

> "Before I accept, would the agency consider a superior-qualifications step determination? My <specific qualifications and accomplishments> exceed the position's requirements in <area>, documented in <evidence>. I understand this must be approved before my start date."

Also ask about a recruitment incentive and annual-leave credit for relevant non-federal experience. Don't cite a competing offer unless HR confirms this agency considers it (decoding.md §8). NHS and UK Civil Service: "Given <years and evidence of relevant experience>, could my starting salary be set above the band minimum? I can send <evidence>."

## 9. Accepting

Only once the final terms are in writing.

> "Hi <name>, I'm delighted to accept the offer for <title, level> on the terms in your letter of <date>: base <figure and currency>, bonus target <%>, <equity units or value, vesting>, sign-on <figure> (<clawback terms>), start date <date>, <location and remote days>. Could you send the revised offer letter for signature, and let me know the next steps for <background check / right to work>? Thank you, <user>"

Checklist before the user signs and resigns: every agreed term in the revised letter; contingencies and how long they take; the clawback text; notice and covenants read; remote arrangement in writing. Then the user withdraws from other processes promptly (job-application-manager drafts the notes).

## 10. Declining

Promptly, decline in the first two lines, no numbers, no negotiation after it:

> "Hi <name>, thank you for the offer and for your time throughout the process. After careful thought, I've decided to <accept another role / stay in my current role>. I've valued meeting <team> and hope our paths cross again. Best, <user>"

Before: "Unfortunately your offer wasn't competitive with another company's package." After: the version above; a comparison adds nothing and burns the bridge.

## 11. A counteroffer from the current employer

Ask the user, before any reply:

1. Was money the only reason for leaving? If not, are the other reasons fixed, in writing?
2. What does staying cost in trust or in the promotion path?
3. Would they really have left? Using an outside offer only as a raise tactic risks both offers.

Statistics such as "80% who accept a counteroffer leave within 6 months" can't be traced to a study; don't quote them. If they stay, decline the new offer promptly (§10).

## 12. Reneging

Advise strongly against accepting with any intention of withdrawing. If the user must: do it at once, by phone or video first and then in writing, apologise, blame no one, offer to repay any bonus, and check the contract's notice and clawback terms (in the UK an accepted offer can already be a binding contract; countries.md §5). Don't post about it.

## 13. Lint for every draft

- Every number, date and company traces to the user or a cited source, with its currency.
- One consolidated counter, 2–4 asks, each with a reason; no "one more thing" later.
- No invented or inflated offers, deadlines, forfeitures or "industry standard" figures.
- No apology for asking, no ultimatum, no comparison that insults the employer.
- No announced priorities ("base matters most"); packages instead.
- Under about 200 words for a counter email; plain, warm, specific.
- A written recap follows every call.
