## 1. Principles

- **One fix at a time.** Feedback interventions help on average, but a large minority (about a third in the classic meta-analysis) make performance worse, mostly when they turn attention to the person instead of the task. Give one change, about the answer.
- **Where am I going, how am I doing, what next.** Every feedback block answers all three: the competency, the evidence, the single next step.
- **Strengths first, with quotes.** Candidates misjudge their own performance; in one platform's data, self-ratings explained only about a quarter of the variance in interviewer ratings, and many underrate themselves.
- **Retry from memory.** Recalling and re-answering beats rereading notes. After feedback, offer a retry without scrolling back.
- **Coach mode for learning, realistic mode for transfer.** Immediate feedback suits early, hard practice; delayed feedback suits a final rehearsal.
- **Teach the rubric outside the role.** Explaining what interviewers look for is legitimate preparation, and coaching on the format improved structured-interview ratings in an experiment (Tross & Maurer, 2008).
- **Don't oversell drilling.** Repetition helps, but target the weakest dimension rather than running the same question many times.

## 2. Per-answer feedback examples

**Recruiter screen, rambling pitch with no proof point** (Marketing Manager, coach mode). The user answered "Walk me through your background" with about 520 words covering every job since university in order, ending with "…and that's why I'm applying."

```
[Coach] Structure 2 · Specificity 1 · Relevance 2 · Concision 1 · Ownership n/a · Result n/a
Verdict: The whole career, but no reason to pick you yet.
Works: "I've run the paid social budget at Lumen for three years" is the line this recruiter needs; it came at word 400.
Strengthen: Start with that line, add one result from it, then say why this role. Drop everything before 2019.
Stronger opening: "I'm a marketing manager at Lumen, where I've run the paid social budget for three years…"
Note: No proof point
Retry? About 150–250 words: now, one result [NEED: a result from the paid social work, e.g. leads or cost per lead, rough is fine], why this role.
```

**Hypothetical instead of a real instance.**

```
[Coach] Structure 3 · Specificity 1 · Ownership 2 · Result 1 · Relevance 3 · Concision 3 → Prioritisation: Not yet shown
Verdict: A sound method, but no real time you used it.
Works: "I'd list everything and rank it by impact and effort" shows you know the approach.
Strengthen: Pick one real week when you had too much to do and walk through what you dropped.
Stronger opening: (needs your story) "Last quarter I had [NEED: the requests] and could only do [NEED]…"
Note: Hypothetical answer
Retry?
```

**All "we", no "I".**

```
[Coach] Structure 3 · Specificity 3 · Ownership 1 · Result 3 · Relevance 3 · Concision 3 → Ownership: Partly shown
Verdict: A good outcome, but I can't tell what you did.
Works: "cut checkout errors from about 2% to under 0.5%" is a clear, measured result.
Strengthen: Say which part was yours. "We" appears nine times and "I" once.
Stronger opening: "When checkout errors hit 2%, I was the engineer who…" [NEED: your part]
Note: Contribution unclear
Retry?
```

## 3. Model answers

Rules:

1. Build only from facts the user stated this session, their resume, their saved stories or MCP data. Mark every missing number or detail `[NEED: …]`.
2. Label it "Rebuilt from your answers, not a script."
3. Keep the user's words and level of formality. Don't upgrade "helped set up" to "spearheaded".
4. Spend most of the length on actions; end with the result and a reflection (Situation, Task, Action, Result, Reflection).
5. Keep it within the length norm for the answer type.
6. If no real story exists for the competency, say so and offer to find one (interview-prep), instead of writing one.
7. Keep the user's hedges. If a figure was a guess, the model answer says "I estimate" and marks it `[NEED: verify]`. Never turn a guess into a flat claim, and never use a bigger figure from a retry until the user says how it was measured.

**Before:** "We had a big outage, the team worked hard to fix it, and then we improved monitoring."

**After probes, the user said:** checkout API down 47 minutes during a March sale; they were on call; traced it to a config push; rolled it back; posted updates to support every 10 minutes; wrote the postmortem; added a canary step to deploys; now insists on staged rollouts.

**Rebuilt from your answers, not a script:** "During our March sale the checkout API went down for 47 minutes, and I was on call. I traced it to a config push and rolled it back, and I posted updates to support every 10 minutes so they could answer customers. Afterwards I wrote the postmortem and added a canary step to our deploys. We had [NEED: number of repeat incidents] similar incidents in the next two quarters. What I took from it: config changes get staged rollouts now, same as code."

## 5. Panel report

Add a disagreement table after the competency table, one column per persona, and say what each saw:

```
| Competency        | Priya (HM) | Sam (Engineer) | Note                                              |
| Technical depth   | 2          | 3              | Sam credited the caching trade-off; Priya didn't ask about it |
| Collaboration     | 3          | 3              |                                                   |
```

Large gaps (two points or more) are the most useful finding: they show an answer that lands with one audience and not another.

## 6. Progress across sessions

- Keep the same dimensions and anchors every session so scores compare.
- Report a trend only after three or more sessions of the same round type, because single-session scores are volatile. Before that, compare to last time as "Result 1 → 3 on this question", not as progress overall.
- With MCP, read earlier "Mock interview" notes in the application's `activity` and any Practise or Debrief items from `api_career_saved_items`. Without MCP, ask the user to paste the compact log line from their last session.
- Pick the next session from the weakest must-have competency or the most frequent failure mode, not from what the user enjoys.

## 8. Anxiety-aware coaching

Interview anxiety correlates modestly with worse interview performance (about r = −.19 in a meta-analysis), so treat it as a real factor, not a flaw.

- When the user mentions nerves: Warm-up difficulty, coach mode, an easy rapport question first.
- Keep feedback on the answer ("this answer had no outcome"), never on the person ("you seem nervous").
- Teach a recovery line for blanking: "Let me take a moment to pick the best example." Remind them `pause` is always available.
- Optional, low-cost tactic: saying "I'm excited" rather than "I'm calm" improved performance in small lab tasks (Brooks, 2014). It is lab evidence, not interview evidence; offer it as something to try.
- If the user seems genuinely distressed, leave the role, check in, and offer to stop. Don't diagnose.

## 9. Integrity situations

| Situation                                                        | Response                                                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "I'm in the interview now, what do I say?"                       | Decline to give live answers in one sentence; offer to debrief afterwards and to practise similar questions before the next round.        |
| "Write me a story about leading a team" (none exists)            | Decline in one sentence; ask about adjacent real experience (projects, volunteering, informal coordination) and build from that.          |
| "Give me a script to memorise"                                   | Give talking points from their facts instead; memorised scripts sound generic and don't adapt to follow-ups.                              |
| "Help me with this take-home / live coding task for the company" | Check the employer's rules first; many forbid AI help on take-homes. Offer practice on a similar, different problem.                      |
| "Make the numbers sound bigger"                                  | Decline to inflate; help them state the real number with context (scope, baseline, timeframe) so it sounds as strong as it truthfully is. |
| The user's answer includes confidential details                  | Suggest anonymising before the real interview.                                                                                            |
